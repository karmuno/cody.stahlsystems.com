Four Tourists

Cody Jane Stahl

Hurricane Sandy has hit and Manhattan has gone dark. The darkness renders uncanny the city you know so well in the light. You are in this poem.

Poem · ~230 words

One The sloshing of my boots through the rain-damp roads of my hometown betray the uneasiness of my gait. This city never sleeps and these lights never go out. The darkness is a parasite in this town of immigrants engulfing sky-shooting monoliths, the towers of Babel we built despite our baffled tongues. I strut these streets daily but tonight I trip over cracks in the sidewalk. The restaurants have emptied their coolers. The lights go down on Broadway. The Empire State Building glows angelic through the storm. I am a tourist in my city.

Two Kick and pound and scratch at my skin but even if you cut me open, you will never find a boy in there.

Three When we kiss, your lips are dry and cracked. Our abandon tears bloody canals in your mouth. The winter howls at the windows of your car and the same viral strain fills both our noses with thick congesting sweetness. Who else has tasted what you’ve tasted, wrapped me in their bodies to wait out the storm? How many spines have I traced my fingers down? How many weddings have I planned? Who were they to have me? Their names are raindrops, subsumed into the ocean spray. My brain sends “I love you” to my mouth but my tongue stumbles on my two front teeth.

A Priesthood

Cody Jane Stahl

In your life, the only constants are Change and Being. A cold random universe does not preclude God. This poem is a Psalm.

Poem · ~220 words

I kneel at the altar of the nameless To Be, a god of small action, and vast stillness, spirit beyond tense and declension, the eternal infinitive. The Lady of all doing — of the ocean’s heaving tide, of the long-dead star’s devouring blackness, of the machine gun patter of a hummingbird’s heart. I too am the unknowable To Be. The thoughts that flood my brain travel no faster than the light that bubbles slowly up from the nuclear slag furnace at the heart of the sun to fling itself mindless into space. Some will hit our Earth as particles and heat the soil, feed our green breathing sun-eaters. Most will wave on endlessly, skirt the rims of far-flung galaxies, rest not in all the churning of the universe.

The Kingdom of God is like a spaceless point of energy. By some deep stirring it bursts its bounds, emits and annihilates, congeals into matter and lives as orderly a life as totality can live until it stretches itself so thin and cold that all light wanders lonely, that all waveforms stagnate in flux. Two particles quantumly entangled in that emptiness will speak in some way, each to their mate, “I’ll collapse if you do.” But always their fates, however probable, remain uncertain. Physical intimacy in a heat-dead universe.

Can’t Get Started (Astrology)

Cody Jane Stahl

You’re on break from a relationship that you’ve known was ending from the moment it began. Though you have no scientific reason to believe them, you consult the stars. They sing this song.

Song · ~270 words

I can’t get started on anything without dwelling on the end. I can’t fall in love with anyone, ’cause anything I do could lead me there again. I’m just waiting on the shoe to drop, the tables to turn, the wave function to collapse. I can’t take myself anywhere these days without a chaperone to watch my back.

The moon and Mars were married in the sign of Gemini when I sat down to bare myself to waning summer light But you are of December, and I am of July. Never works out well for anyone, no matter how I try.

Now my official stance is that the stars and planets dance alone and any pull they have upon our lives is pure coincidence, but when you got your palm read and she told you were fated to part, even though the rest was bullshit, part of me freaked out.

But in spite of all the augurs, in spite of all the signs, in spite of any pattern in the entrails I divined I didn’t get an answer to what’s burning in my mind. The closest thing’s the love for you I carry all the time. If I still had your number, I’d be tying up your line If we could make it through November without crumbling we’ll be fine, so I won’t write an ending ’til my pen runs dry and all the page reveals are vague impressions on the line

I could dump you in the gutter I could fuse you to my spine I could go home in the morning I could love you till I die

Short story · ~2100 words

“Not yet!” said Sakura, placing a duck on Hana’s head. She insisted that Aguilera got the full spa experience, whatever that meant. “Count to 100!”

But looking into Sakura’s smiling face somehow made the anger pass over and through her, leaving a strange feeling behind — perhaps something like peace.

It was not a feeling that she was used to, and she didn’t know what to do with it. If there was some human response that would have been appropriate here it was nowhere in Hana’s repertoire. Lost for words, she did what came naturally and sent a wave of water directly into Sakura’s face. Old habits and all that.

“You’re such a bully,” she said. When Sakura splashed her back, she devolved into a fit of giggles despite herself.

This too was new. Hana Natsuki had probably laughed more over the past two days than she had in the 25 years she had lived as Aguilera.

The past two days were also the first time Hana had ever been touched with any kind of tenderness. Intimacy was a rare thing for Aguilera, and it most often took the form of violence. The Deadmans she lived with were no companions to her, and Tamaki’s cloying insistence on closeness only drove her away. Even Lord Giff, her promised husband, struck her down when she offered herself to him. Her confidantes had been nothing but liars and demons. In some sense, Hana had never been touched by a human.

Is that why Sakura’s closeness in this moment felt so strange?

Their first embrace had been a breaking of the dam, when the decades-old knot of hate and fear and envy and nameless yearning came undone all at once, and Hana cried real human tears for the first time since childhood. Sakura held her then while years of pent-up emotion poured down her cheeks onto Sakura’s back. It was one of those moments that rattles your worldview and turns you inside out until you come up for air and find yourself a different person. It might be called a “healing moment,” but Hana didn’t have those words.

Since then she and Sakura had been in each other’s presence near constantly. Sakura insisted that Hana had “a lot of catching up to do” when it came to being human. So they did the works: clothes shopping, ice cream, movies, karaoke, walking through the park just to walk. Through it all Hana grew accustomed to Sakura’s touch – the same touch that broke her world apart two days ago. By now it was almost commonplace. Not boring, not shocking. Familiar.

Then why did Hana feel so strange in this bath? Sakura had strolled along with her hand wrapped around Hana’s waist a dozen times. Why, whenever one of their hands brushed against the other’s side under the water, did Hana feel dizzy?

Sakura rolled her eyes, “and I promise I’ll buy you ice cream as soon as we’re done.” Hana’s face lit up and Sakura’s heart skipped a beat.

“In that case,” said Hana, “I’m all yours!”

“As if,” said Sakura. “It’s just that our place isn’t exactly equipped for a full luxury experience, and I wanted to make sure that you’d be comfortable, and that –” her voice hitched almost imperceptibly “we wouldn’t get interrupted.”

Sakura laughed. “Well, as much luxury as I can give you. I’m not exactly trained, but I thought with everything you’ve been through you might prefer somebody you trusted over a total stranger.” Sakura’s eyes showed a sad kindness.

“Prefer for what?” Hana had been full of questions for the past two days, and she was showing no sign of slowing.

Sakura presented a bottle of coconut oil from the drawer of her nightstand. “For a massage!”

Hana’s blood rushed to her cheeks. She didn’t know why. It caused Sakura to hesitate. “Is that ok?” she asked. “I’m sorry, maybe it’s too intimate. I got ahead of myself. Don’t worry about it, I’ll put it away and we’ll just get –”

Our Holy Mother

Cody Jane Stahl

You blink your eyes open from a dream and you can still recall the old gray church and the Passion that engulfed it. Your dream becomes this story.

Short story · ~2600 words

In another time, Holy Mother, the graying stone church on the corner of Maynard and Rose, boasted tinted glass windows adorned with the images of saints and angels that looked out over the congregation and the alleyway behind. But with years came hail and baseballs and rocks, and management decided that enough is enough, and had the rest of the windows broken, replacing them with dull yellow panes that blocked out the sun and invited no mischief. Since then, even those simple windows have been boarded up, and the Holy Mother is now blind to the world.

Today, a butcher prays in the pews. He is alone for the time being, but for the Great Stone Cross that has always protruded from the sanctuary floor. The Cross once crucified a life-sized porcelain Jesus, but since the mothers threw a fit over the depiction of violence in the Church, it now holds only the nails that pierced his hands and feet. There is no spot in the nave from which its presence cannot be felt. Back when clergymen preached fire and brimstone and forgiveness to a crowded congregation, the Cross, with its beaten and bloodied Messiah, could move a non-believer to conversion as though it were Christ in the flesh. For added effect, an altar boy often shone a great floodlight from the rear of the center aisle, casting a thirty-foot shadow of the instrument of torture on the walls and the windows behind. From the outside, the saints and angels would glow as if they were ascending.

But not today. The only lights in the church are the dim overheads that the city had ordered them to install for candlelight ceremonies. Lights that hardly cast shadows. The butcher had come at two-thirty in the afternoon. He had to knock four times before he was let in by the doorman, still dressed in last night’s jeans and t-shirt, and his dog. The dog is an old black lab and the doorman is young, maybe thirty. He told the visitor that he was surprised that he had come so early on a Friday and the visitor said I’d just like to sit alone for a while and so he did.

It is five o’clock, and the butcher hasn’t moved from his pew, except one time to use the bathroom and another to throw away his used tissues and grab another box. For the first time since he’s arrived, the dog and the doorkeeper break his solitude by walking in and taking a seat by the altar. This is the only entrance, the atrium having caved in last winter, and this is where, fifteen minutes later, a teenage couple walks in speaking Spanish to each other. They give the doorkeeper some bills, and proceed to an inconspicuous corner. In the almost darkness they are bold, and the man with tissues watches. The doorkeeper watches. The dog tries to sleep. The boy has his hair in cornrows and his jeans around his ankles. The girl wears a golden cross between her breasts. They make love.

At five-twenty a family of five enter, three kids and two Mommies. The kids are running up and down the aisles and all through the pews. The Latino couple stops when a little boy almost spots them. Jeans are pulled up, hair is straightened, and they restrict themselves to mouths for the remainder of their visit. The Mommies sit near the altar and try their best to contain the mass of child that they have brought along.

In the next ten minutes, the congregation grows from eight to twenty, with each newcomer slipping some cash into the doorkeeper’s hand and taking their pick of the pews. At five-thirty-five, a man and woman in their sixties come in and sit down near the butcher. The latter looks up and says something in Yiddish, to which the woman responds What, so he apologizes and blows his nose and never speaks to them again.

For twenty minutes, the congregation doubles in number. The forty of them, all giddy and expectant, do not come close to filling the chamber. None speak of the Cross.

At five-fifty, the doorkeeper closes his door and leads the dog to a small closet, from which he produces an old sixteen-millimeter film projector. At five-fifty-three, the projector has been assembled and placed in the rear of the center aisle. At five-fifty-four, the first of ten reels of film has been loaded into the projector. At five-fifty-eight, the dog sits down in the back. At five-fifty-nine, the lights are extinguished, and there is total darkness. There is chatter among the congregation. Women talk about men. Men talk about wars. Children talk. At six o’clock, the projector starts up, a hush falls over the congregation. There Is Light.

The light shatters the darkness. The only notable sounds in the vacuous space are the click-click-click of the projector and the excited panting of the dog, whose gaze is fixed upon the now illuminated wall, the light broken into four distinct patches by the shadow of the Great Stone Cross. Aside from this, there is a human silence, filled with breathing and heartbeats and shifting seats and muffled ahems. And the silence of the Church, a true silence, for the Holy Mother stopped settling years ago.

In seconds, the pure white light gives way to a new darkness, and then a countdown, from Five to Two. A man appears in black and white, draped in an overcoat and ambling towards the congregation. He has been shot, and it is his fault, and he promises to tell all. Later in the film, this man will die, but now he speaks into a dictation machine. He is sorry, so sorry, for what he’s done, but he’s done it for a woman. And all this time he is projected, broken into quarters, on the wall of the Holy Mother.

The dog has been here since before anyone can remember. If you talk to anybody that’s been around long enough, you might hear stories of when the dog was a pup. When the dog would bark at intruders and sniff out those who soiled the pews with their lust. Nobody actually knew the dog back then. This was when they still preached forgiveness in the Holy Mother, when these pews were full. Maybe even before then, depending on whom you ask. Some people doubted it was even the same dog that came to the church so many years ago, starving with his tail between his legs, or in the handbasket of a popular Belgian noblewoman. For this dog to be that dog would require this dog to be at least sixty years old. For this dog to be that dog would be absurd. But this is the dog that is here now, and right now he is watching a man smoke a cigarette with his mistress.

They have just had sex, but nobody saw. When this film was made there were rules against that sort of thing. Scenes of Passion should not be introduced when not essential to the plot. Instead he fastens his tie. She adjusts her skirt. They kiss in the darkness. For one second. Two seconds. Three seconds. Done.

By now the projector has gone through two reels of film. A dark spot flashes in the upper right quadrant of the screen, and the doorkeeper lines up the next reel. A few seconds later, there is another flash of darkness, and the image bursts into color.

The man and his mistress are gone now. In their stead is another man, lamb white robes covering sunburnt skin. The dog sits up straight, as if it has just heard its master come home.

The merchants and the usurers are desecrating the house of God, or so says the man. They scatter like roaches from his whips and cries. On the wall behind the Cross, their faces are displayed one by one. They are frightened and sweaty. Their features cast the pronounced shadows of Very Bad Men. They are backed into a corner and they are now seen, as a mass, subdivided into four sections by the shadow of the Great Stone Cross – a Brady Bunch of sin.

Though this is not what they came for, there is no reaction in the congregation to this sudden change. It has been thirty minutes since people began to move across the wall, and by now everyone has adjusted to the flood of light. The human silence has grown less. Some of the children are growing restless, and one family leaves through the door. When they do, there is a hole in the image as part of the scene momentarily projects itself onto the street beyond. In the pews, a babe suckles at his mother’s breast. The Mommies hush their child-mass. The door closes, and silence is restored.

Here and now, the children belong to the Mommies, though there is nothing in their appearance that indicates this. They come in all shapes and colors. In a crowded room, their proximity would appear as nothing more than chance. But here in the sparseness of the Holy Mother, when a child speaks up and the Mommies say shh, it is enough to know that he is theirs.

The reels are changed again, and the color disappears. The man and woman are back. She is wearing dark sunglasses. If she is seen with him, her husband will be furious. Despite the transformation, the dog does not relax. Its interest has been piqued.

Five rows behind the Mommies, the butcher is still in tears. Though he looks towards the front of the chamber, he does not see the image. His eyes focus on the Cross. He is aware of the film only through the sound of the dialogue, the constant click-click-click, and the flickering forms that pass between quadrants. His body heaves and shakes with muted sobs. He has run out of tissues, and his shirt sleeves are now wet and sticky with the brood of his sadness.

The man and the woman are on a train now. Her husband has stepped outside to have a cigarette. Another unessential Scene of Passion. The man hobbles towards the back of the car, away from the congregation. He and the husband share a smoke. They chat, they laugh, and as planned, the husband falls and is swallowed by the darkness and the Cross.

In the rear of the congregation the projector click-click-clicks away. In a pile on the floor are three reels, fresh and virginal. In a second pile are six reels, one of which is still warm from the friction and the light of the old projector. The remaining reel is turning, the projector tugging at its cellulose nitrate film at a foot-and-a-half-per-second. It is nearly empty, and a few minutes later, the doorkeeper removes it, placing it atop the second pile. There is one less virgin reel.

There is color once more.

On the wall, there is a family, weeping over a crumpled form. Angry tears. The man in robes is too late, he cannot help them. But lo! A miracle! The dead man walks! Our bodies are whole! We are blind no more! The tears become joyful, and the sobs become cries of triumph and adulation. Soon the faces on the wall change, and the scene is over. No characters cry, no sadness lingers, no color remains. But sobs are still heard. In the middle of the congregation, the butcher sits, eyes moist, the pile of used tissues a barrier between himself and the world. The elderly couple beside him whisper to each other. They worry that the man will attract the dog. That dog hates to see men cry, the woman says. Neither of them want to deal with the disturbance that that old black lab will cause.

But the dog does not come. It is oblivious to the congregation. It sees only the wall.

The man and woman are once again projected. There are tears. They are cried by the woman, but they are cried for her husband, so they are false. These tears are beautiful tears. Not like the tears she cries later, when she is alone with the man. When she holds the gun in her hand. Not like the tears the butcher cries when their kiss is obstructed by the Cross, where their final moment of tenderness is crucified for one second. Two seconds. Three seconds. Done.

For the final reel, color returns to the wall of the Holy Mother. The man prays in a garden. He tells the watch to stay alert, but they cannot. It is too late. Unconscious, they let a friend approach. He kisses the man on the cheek. The quintessence of betrayal. In a dark corner of the nave, two Latine youths kiss on the mouth. He with cornrows, she with a cross between her breasts.

The butcher cries.

They take the man to a judge. He is tried. He is mocked. Who died and made you King? He is condemned. The Mommies struggle to keep the children on their best behavior. The children squirm. He is condemned.

The butcher cries.

A soldier whips the man and red blood comes out. He has this wreath, this thorny crown, and more blood. Through it all, screams, anguish. Tears. The elderly couple clasp hands, their knuckles grow white, and they find comfort in each other.

The butcher cries.

Soldiers lift a wooden cross onto the man’s shoulders. He carries it at an angle, so that its lowest end drags along the ground. This cross, together with the shadow cast by the Great Stone one, form a Great Asterisk on the wall, as if somewhere there can be found some footnote, some Great Addendum, that will explain it all. The dog is unwavering.

The butcher cries.

As the man trudges through the streets, the ridicule grows louder. There are jeers and taunts and saliva. A woman, the Mother, comes forward from the crowd, but she is thrown back into the sea of limbs and expression. The door opens. It closes. Nobody enters. Nobody leaves.

The butcher cries.

There is a hill that the man must ascend, and he is so tired. From the sea of jeering faces there coalesce two figures. One lays him down on the ground. The other hammers nails into hands and feet. With each stroke the projector seems to jump, click-click-CLACK-click-click-CLACK, as if flinching from the images. With each stroke, a jolt goes through the boards over the windows. Cracks form. Slivers of a crucifixion bleed through into the darkness beyond.

The butcher cries.

There is white and only white on the wall, but the figure on the Cross remains. The projector protests, CLACK-CLACK-CLACK-CLACK. He is gazing at the Heavens and the congregation is gazing at him, and that dog, that dog is transfixed. His mouth is open, dry, pleading. In the back of the congregation, the film catches and burns. There is fire, and yet the figure remains.

The butcher cries.

The film clatters on and clatters on and clatters on and silence falls. The human silence and the church silence melt together. There is no more clatter. There is no more sobbing. There is no more panting. The man, the Man in the Flesh, bellows, screams, cries.

Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani?

There is clatter. There is sobbing. There is panting. There is the sound of splintering wood as the cracks grow and spread over the boarded windows. They shatter and at last the light floods forth into the darkness, into the world, into the city, through eyes long-dead.

And that graying stone church on the corner of Maynard and Rose is filled with Mothers and Sons and Ghosts. And lo! A miracle! Our Holy Mother Is Blind No More!
